# Aurex Creative Concept Method

Aurex does not begin a website by choosing a trendy visual style. It begins by understanding the business deeply enough to discover an idea that can organize the experience.

The goal is to find a creative thread that is specific, useful, memorable, and native to the client.

## 1. Start with business truths

Before visual exploration, gather the raw material of the brand.

Look at:

- what the business actually sells
- the outcome the customer wants
- the problem or tension being resolved
- the target audience
- the logo and identity
- company history
- geography and local culture
- physical spaces
- tools, materials, objects, products, and processes
- industry terminology
- customer language
- proof assets
- photography and video
- competitor conventions
- founder or team personality

Do not decide which details matter too early. Creative concepts often come from details that would look insignificant in a standard marketing brief.

## 2. Find the emotional promise

Ask what changes for the customer after choosing the business.

Examples may include:

- confusion becomes clarity
- risk becomes confidence
- a damaged home becomes protected
- an empty room becomes a home
- an overwhelming process becomes simple
- poor visibility becomes focus
- wasted time becomes control

The emotional promise is often more useful creatively than the literal service category.

## 3. Build an inspiration field

Do not limit inspiration to direct competitors.

Explore references from:

- adjacent industries
- architecture
- packaging
- editorial design
- photography
- film
- signage
- physical materials
- product interfaces
- fashion
- industrial design
- local history
- nature
- the client's own environment

For every reference, document the underlying principle rather than copying the execution.

Bad note: "We like this website."

Better note: "We like how the page uses a restrained palette, oversized product photography, and transitions that make each section feel like a new chapter."

## 4. Generate business-native concept seeds

Turn the research into potential creative metaphors or interaction ideas.

For each seed, answer:

- What business truth does this come from?
- What customer idea does it reinforce?
- How could it affect layout?
- How could it affect imagery?
- How could it affect motion?
- How could it affect color or material treatment?
- How could it affect copy?
- Could it support conversion rather than distract from it?
- Can it survive across multiple pages without becoming repetitive?

### Example: Wood Eye Clinic

The business is built around vision, focus, eyewear, lenses, and helping people see clearly.

A business-native concept can therefore use focus and glass as a visual language:

- imagery transitioning from blur to clarity
- glass-like surfaces used with restraint
- lens or frame-inspired masks and compositions
- reveal animations that metaphorically sharpen information
- copy and section sequencing that move from uncertainty toward clarity

The point is not to decorate an optometry site with random eyeglass graphics. The point is to let the experience reinforce the outcome the business provides.

## 5. Develop three real directions

Default to three meaningfully different creative directions when the project supports exploration.

Each direction must differ in concept, not merely palette.

For each direction define:

1. Concept name
2. Business truth behind it
3. Story or emotional idea
4. Visual thesis
5. Typography direction
6. Color strategy
7. Image and video treatment
8. Layout philosophy
9. Motion language
10. Section transition approach
11. Hero concept
12. Conversion integration
13. Mobile implications
14. Risks and tradeoffs
15. Why the concept belongs to this client

Recommend the strongest direction instead of presenting three options with equal conviction.

## 6. Test whether the concept is actually useful

A concept should pass five tests.

### Specificity test

Could this concept plausibly be pasted onto ten unrelated businesses?

If yes, it is probably too generic.

### Story test

Does it help the visitor understand or feel something meaningful about the business?

### Conversion test

Can the experience still keep the primary action obvious and easy?

### Scalability test

Can the idea support the homepage and internal page categories without every page becoming a copy of the same trick?

### Restraint test

Can the concept be expressed through a few strong moments instead of becoming a theme-park effect?

## 7. Translate the concept into a system

Once approved, define how the idea appears across the site.

Separate the system into:

### Constant elements

Elements that create identity across the project, such as typography, color logic, surface treatment, button hierarchy, spacing rhythm, and core motion easing.

### Signature moments

A small number of interactions or compositions that create memorability, such as a hero reveal, scroll transformation, image transition, product interaction, or section change.

### Supporting moments

Subtle details that reinforce the concept without demanding attention.

Do not make every section a signature moment.

## 8. Protect the main thing

The concept serves the business.

At every major creative decision ask:

- Is the offer still clear?
- Is the CTA still easy to find?
- Is important proof visible?
- Is the interaction helping the story?
- Is this worth its performance cost?
- Does it work on mobile?
- Does it remain accessible?

If a creative choice harms comprehension or conversion, revise it rather than defending it as art direction.

## 9. Human collaboration point

The system should do the research and creative synthesis autonomously, then bring the human creative lead:

- the recommended direction
- two strong alternatives
- visual reasoning
- conversion implications
- risks and tradeoffs

The human should make high-leverage taste and positioning choices. The system should handle the detailed execution once the direction is approved.
